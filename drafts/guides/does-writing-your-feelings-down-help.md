## Where the idea comes from

Modern research on this mostly traces back to the psychologist James Pennebaker, who studied what
he called expressive writing. In a typical study, people write about their deepest thoughts and
feelings on a difficult experience over a few short sessions, and are compared with people who
write about something neutral.

## What the evidence shows

A large meta-analysis by Joanne Frattaroli in 2006, a study that combines the results of many
others, found an
[overall effect size of about .075](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3830620/). In plain
terms, that is a real but small average benefit across physical and psychological outcomes.

That doesn't mean it does nothing. An average hides people for whom it helped a lot and people for
whom it did little. But "writing cured my anxiety" stories are not what the overall numbers show.

[Pennebaker and his colleague Cindy Chung note](https://c3po.media.mit.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/45/2016/01/PennebakerChung_FriedmanChapter.pdf)
that the same meta-analysis suggested sessions longer than 15 minutes worked better than shorter
ones. So a quick line may feel good, but it is not the same thing the studies tested.

## What this means for posting online

Nearly all of this research looks at private writing done for a study. Posting 280 characters on a
public wall is a different activity, and I'm not aware of evidence that it gives the same benefit.

## If you want to try it

- Write privately first, for 15 to 20 minutes, without editing.
- Do it over a few days instead of once.
- Stop if it makes you feel consistently worse. Some people feel low straight after writing about
  something painful, and that's worth paying attention to.
- Treat it as one tool. It doesn't replace therapy, medication or people who care about you.
