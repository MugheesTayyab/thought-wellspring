## The online disinhibition effect

In 2004 the psychologist John Suler described what he called the
[online disinhibition effect](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15257832/): some people disclose
more, or act out more, online than they would in person. He named six factors that interact with
each other, and three of them are easy to recognise:

- **Anonymity.** What you write can't be traced back to the rest of your life, so you feel less
  exposed.
- **Invisibility.** You can't see a frown, a raised eyebrow, or someone looking away. Those small
  signals shape what we're willing to say face to face.
- **Delay.** You don't have to sit through someone's reaction. You can say it and leave.

## Why telling a friend is harder

A friend isn't just a listener. They know your mother, they'll see you next week, and they'll
remember. Telling them something changes how they might see you, and you have to live with that.

An anonymous post costs almost none of that. [YOU: Does this match your own experience? Where

That doesn't make anonymous sharing better than real friendship. A wall can't check on you
tomorrow. For some people, writing it anonymously is simply a first step that makes the real
conversation easier to imagine.

## The same effect brings out the worst

Suler called the two sides *benign* and *toxic* disinhibition. The conditions that let someone
admit a painful truth also let someone else write something cruel, and they can feel equally
freeing to the person typing.
